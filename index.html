About

I am a Research Scientist in Computing & Data Sciences at Brookhaven National Laboratory, working on quantum computing and quantum machine learning. My training is in analysis: my Ph.D. at the University of Maryland, with Pierre-Emmanuel Jabin, was on dispersion and averaging in transport equations, mixing of incompressible flows, and memory-driven models of animal migration.

Since then I have carried the same tools into machine learning (optimal transport for speech, variational analysis of deep networks, neural forecasting of chaotic geophysical systems) and into quantum computing, where I treat the measurement of a quantum circuit as something to be learned rather than fixed.

Before Brookhaven I was an Assistant Professor of Mathematics and Computer Science at Seton Hall University, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Colorado Boulder, Academia Sinica and the University of Montana, and a data scientist at Bank of America. I review for ICML, ICLR and IEEE journals and conferences.

One thread, five chapters

Each project below asks how something is carried: a density along velocities, fluid along streamlines, an animal along what it remembers, probability mass from noisy to clean data, and a quantum observable along a unitary path. The chapters run in the order I worked on them.

§1

Averaging lemmas for kinetic transport

Ph.D. thesis, Chapter 2; with P.-E. Jabin and E. Tadmor

A density \(f(t,x,v)\) of particles at position \(x\) with velocity \(v\) can be as rough as its data. Its velocity averages are better behaved: they gain fractional derivatives in \(x\). These averaging lemmas are a key source of compactness in kinetic theory and of regularity for conservation laws.

$$\partial_t f + a(v)\cdot\nabla_x f = (-\Delta_v)^{\kappa/2} g$$ $$\rho_\varphi(t,x)=\int f(t,x,v)\,\varphi(v)\,dv \;\in\; L^2\big([0,T],H^{s}_{x,\mathrm{loc}}\big)$$

Given \(f\in L^\infty_tL^{p_1}_xL^{p_2}_v\), \(g\in L^1_tL^{q_1}_xL^{q_2}_v\) and a non-degenerate \(a\in \mathrm{Lip}\), the average lies in \(H^s\) for every \(s\) below an explicit exponent \(S(p_i,q_i,\kappa,\gamma)\).

We introduced a commutator method: pick a multiplier \(Q\) so that its commutator \([B,Q]\) with the transport operator \(B=a(v)\cdot\nabla_x\) is positive and gains a derivative, the mechanism behind local smoothing for dispersive equations. The proof needs only elementary Fourier analysis, covers a new range of integrability assumptions, and gives a direct regularity result for measure-valued solutions of scalar conservation laws in one space dimension.

Analysis & PDE 15 (2022), arXiv:2003.05047, Séminaire Laurent Schwartz (2021)

§2

When incompressible flows cannot mix

Ph.D. thesis, Chapter 3

A divergence-free field on the two-torus is the rotated gradient of a stream function, and the transport equation \(\partial_t f+\operatorname{div}(af)=0\) inherits a Hamiltonian structure: \(\psi\) is constant along every trajectory.

$$a=\nabla^{\perp}\psi=(\partial_2\psi,\,-\partial_1\psi),\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^2}\Phi\big(\psi(x)\big)\,f(t,x)\,dx=0 .$$

Using this, I showed that a flow is unmixing whenever \(\psi\) has a local maximum or minimum, and that the property survives any perturbation of \(\psi\) that is small in sup norm. Taking \(a\) to be a random Fourier series turns this into a quantitative, probabilistic statement about when mixing fails, read off from Fourier coefficients.

Thesis: Dispersion Properties of Transport Equations and Applications, University of Maryland, 2020.

§3

Memory and migration

Ph.D. thesis, Chapter 4; with W. F. Fagan and P.-E. Jabin

Animals return to places they remember. I modeled each memory channel as a field \(M_i(t,x)\) that fades nonlinearly, reaching zero in finite time, and is refreshed by what the animal perceives through a kernel \(K\) centered at its position \(X(t)\). The animal then moves by optimal control: a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman value function built from how it evaluates each location.

$$\partial_t M_i=\kappa_i\Big[-d_i\,\operatorname{sgn}(M_i)\sqrt{|M_i|}+u_i\,K\big(X(t),x\big)\big(E(t,x)-M_i\big)\Big]$$ $$H(t,x)=\sum_i w_i\,M_i(t,x)$$

\(E\): environment quality, \(d_i,u_i\): decay and update rates, \(w_i\): weights that combine the channels into the evaluation \(H\).

Simulations in a periodic two-habitat environment show what memory must provide: periodic migration needs both short-term and long-term memory, with time scales in the right order and the ability to weigh one against the other. At the University of Montana I applied the same modeling habits to Chronic Wasting Disease, combining contact-network structure with stochastic models and data-driven inference.

Journal of Theoretical Biology 508 (2021)

§4

Learning with transport, variations and dynamics

2020–2025: CU Boulder, Academia Sinica, Bank of America, Seton Hall

In machine learning I keep reaching for analysis. For speech enhancement without clean labels, I moved noisy feature distributions onto clean ones with discriminator-constrained optimal transport (NeurIPS 2021). To explain domain adaptation, a layer variational analysis treats fine-tuning as a variational problem across layers (ICLR 2023). Stochastic neighbor embedding with an α-divergence decomposes into attractive and repulsive forces (IEEE Access 2024).

$$\min_{T:\;T_{\#}\mu=\nu}\;\int c\big(x,T(x)\big)\,d\mu(x)$$

Monge's problem: the cheapest way to push the distribution \(\mu\) onto \(\nu\).

For chaotic geophysical systems I combined recurrent networks with data assimilation for scalable state estimation and forecasting (JAMES 2022) and developed Fourier reservoir computing for multi-scale quasi-geostrophic dynamics. Recent work covers retrieval-augmented prompt learning against LLM hallucination (IEEE/ACM TASLP), contrastive diffusion models, and multi-channel speech enhancement.

NeurIPS 2021, ICLR 2023, IEEE Access 2024, JAMES 2022, TASLP

§5

Learnable measurements for quantum neural networks

2024–present: Brookhaven National Laboratory and collaborators

A variational quantum circuit (VQC) encodes data, applies a trainable unitary \(U(\theta)\), and measures a fixed observable, usually a Pauli \(Z\). In the Heisenberg picture the observable moves instead of the state, so training traces a path \(H(\theta)=U^\dagger(\theta)H_0U(\theta)\) in the space \(\mathbb{H}(n)\) of Hermitian matrices. Why not let the measurement learn too?

$$f_{\theta,\phi}(x)=\big\langle\psi_x\big|\,U^\dagger(\theta)\,\widetilde H(\phi)\,U(\theta)\,\big|\psi_x\big\rangle,\qquad \widetilde H(\phi)\in\mathbb{H}(k),\;k\le n$$

\(\widetilde H(\phi)\) is a trainable \(k\)-local Hermitian observable with \(4^k\) real parameters. A fixed observable is the special case, so VQC \(\subsetneq\) ANO.

Adaptive non-local observables (ANO) enlarge the functions a circuit can represent without adding depth. Diagonal ANO keeps that flexibility with far fewer parameters. A quasi-polar decomposition splits a trained model into a radial part (spectrum) and an angular part (Lie algebra), so its training trajectory can be read geometrically. Observable geometry uses unitary orbits, stabilizers and homogeneous spaces to count a circuit's effective degrees of freedom.

Applications so far: quantum reinforcement learning, quantum super-resolution, transfer learning of VQCs, learned measurement programs, and the Quantum Gradient Class Activation Map for interpreting hybrid models.

ANO, QCE 2025, QRL, QCE 2025, ICASSP 2026, DANO, Quasi-polar, Observable geometry
